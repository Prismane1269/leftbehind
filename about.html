The brief, the build and the sources

A story about hardware that cannot come home.

This site answers one 2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge brief in full: tell the story of the equipment NASA has left on the Moon, on Mars and in deep space, and make it make sense to school-age space fans. Here is exactly how we did it — and what is real, what is measured, and what is drawn.

01 · the brief

What the challenge asked for

“Since the 1960s, NASA has left hardware across the solar system — on the Moon, on Mars, and in deep space. From rovers and instruments that completed their science missions to probes still traveling away from Earth, this hardware varies widely in both its purpose and current level of functionality. Your challenge is to tell the story of some or all of this equipment that introduces school-age space enthusiasts to the hardware and the science it made possible.”

Challenge brief, 2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge — “Abandoned but not Forgotten: Storytelling about NASA's Discarded Equipment on the Moon and Mars”. Difficulty: Intermediate. Subjects: Astrophysics, Planets & Moons, Space Exploration.

02 · how we answered it

Five decisions shaped this build

  • Every machine is modelled in code. Sixteen Three.js builders draw the actual hardware — descent stages, the wire-wheeled lunar rover, both Mars rover families, InSight's round solar panels, Voyager's dish and RTGs. No model files, no photos standing in for objects.
  • Nothing loads from the network at runtime. Three.js is vendored, textures ship with the site, fonts are a system stack, and every diagram is drawn live. Open it on a laptop in a school with no internet and it still works.
  • Written down to the reader. Short sentences, no jargon without a definition, and an eight-word glossary for the technical terms that cannot be avoided. Two of the six museum machines exist mainly to explain how things work.
  • The physics is computed, not quoted. Light-time and Mars distance are worked out in your browser for today's date, so the numbers change as Earth and Mars move.
  • Every fact is traceable. Each of the 18 machines carries its own NASA sources, printed inside its story. The full list is below.

What is real

Coordinates, dates, distances travelled, mission statistics and mission status — all from NASA mission pages, the NASA Space Science Data Coordinated Archive and NASA fact sheets. Each object's story links to its sources.

What is measured live

The Earth–Mars distance and the radio one-way light time are computed in your browser from a simplified Keplerian model using JPL mean orbital elements. It is good to roughly one percent — plenty to show why a Mars conversation always has a delay, but not a navigation tool.

The distance to the Moon is its mean value (384,400 km) because the real distance wobbles between about 356,500 km and 406,700 km over each month.

What is drawn, not photographed

The Sol 1222 marsquake animation uses a synthetic trace shaped to match the structure of the real event (P-wave, S-wave, surface waves) — InSight's own seismic data is not bundled here. The Ingenuity downlink log is a faithful dramatisation of the real final sols, not a verbatim transcript.

03 · accessibility and reading level

Built so a ten-year-old can drive it

Motion

Two levels, switched from the nav and remembered: Balanced (default) keeps the story scrollable but draws the hero only at spaced 3D waypoints and shows one detailed hall model at a time; Calm freezes the hero canvas, removes spin and parallax, and uses a 20 fps budget while the story and hall still scroll normally. System-level prefers-reduced-motion starts in Calm. No sound autoplays and nothing flashes.

Keyboard and touch

The catalogue, story modals (Esc, ← →), filters and the listening room are fully operable without a mouse. The 3D viewers are drag-and-turn, and every 3D object also exists as text.

If 3D fails

On a device without WebGL the scenes step aside and the readouts fall back to plain values — the whole story is still readable, searchable and sourced.

    Things people ask

    Weight. The descent stage carried the landing engine, the legs and the fuel tanks — almost two tonnes. Flying that back to orbit would have needed far more fuel, so the ascent stage lifted off alone, leaving the base, the ladder and a plaque behind on purpose.
